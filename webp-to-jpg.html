When should you use JPG?

JPG is widely supported by photo viewers, office tools, image editors, and online services. It can be useful when a service does not accept WebP files.

JPG also uses lossy compression. Converting WebP to JPG may change image quality and does not preserve transparency; transparent areas are typically flattened during conversion.

Quality, file size, and transparency

The quality control balances visual detail against output file size. Lower quality can create a smaller file but may introduce visible compression artifacts, especially around text, edges, and fine patterns. Higher quality usually retains more detail but creates a larger file.

JPG does not support transparency. If the source WebP has transparent areas, inspect the output carefully because those areas cannot remain transparent in a JPG file.

Example workflow: making a WebP image compatible with an older form

Some upload forms, office applications, and older image editors accept JPG but reject WebP. Convert a copy of the WebP image to JPG, then open the result in the application that needs it. Check the image dimensions and make sure the subject is still framed correctly.

Transparency needs a deliberate check

JPG cannot store transparent pixels. If the WebP contains a transparent logo or product cut-out, the transparent areas must be flattened against a background in the JPG output. Inspect the edges and background carefully; a logo that looked correct on a dark page may look different when placed on white.

Avoid unnecessary repeated conversions

Both WebP and JPG can use lossy compression. Converting a lossy WebP to JPG may add another generation of compression and cannot restore detail already lost in the WebP. Keep the original WebP and, whenever possible, convert from the highest-quality original image instead.

Understand what changes during WebP to JPG conversion

JPG is widely supported by older image editors, office applications, upload forms, and devices. It can be a practical compatibility format when a service rejects WebP. The conversion creates a new raster image; it does not restore the original source file or increase its resolution. If the WebP was already saved with lossy compression, converting it to JPG may add another generation of compression.

JPG does not support transparency. If the WebP contains transparent pixels, those areas must be filled with a background colour during conversion. Inspect the result around logos, icons, product cut-outs, and other subjects that were designed to sit on a transparent background before using the JPG elsewhere.

Select quality for the intended use

For a quick preview or a small image shared in a message, a smaller-file setting may be sufficient. Choose a higher setting when fine details matter, but remember that a higher quality usually increases file size and cannot restore detail already lost in the source. Compare both the visual result and file size rather than assuming one setting is best for every image.

Check the converted image

  • Open the JPG and inspect edges where transparent areas were flattened.
  • Check small text, gradients, and fine patterns for compression artifacts.
  • Confirm the dimensions are suitable for the app or website that rejected WebP.
  • Keep the original WebP if transparency or future editing may be important.

If you are converting images for a website, consider whether the site supports WebP before replacing the original. WebP may be more efficient for web delivery, while JPG remains useful where compatibility is the priority.
